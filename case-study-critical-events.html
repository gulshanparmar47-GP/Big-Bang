The Problem

History is usually taught as a list of dates to memorise.

That framing hides the more interesting truth: these events form a chain — the control of fire enabled bigger brains, brains enabled language, language enabled everything after. This module was designed to teach not just what happened, but how each breakthrough made the next one possible — and to leave the learner with a framework they can keep, rather than a timeline they'll forget. It covers 16 breakthroughs across roughly a million years, from the control of fire to the internet.

Design Rationale

Turning a list into a framework

01

A framework, not a list

Every event is framed through one of three forces: did it help us survive, share knowledge, or shift power? Giving the learner this lens up front turns sixteen separate facts into one pattern they can actually retain.

02

The compounding chain

Each screen connects explicitly to the next — a bigger brain sets up language, writing sets up formal education. This models causation over memorisation: the harder, more transferable cognitive skill.

03

A check that asks for judgment

The module closes on a synthesis question with no single obvious answer, prompting the learner to apply the three-force framework rather than recall a fact. An earlier recall check confirms the sequence first.

Working with the tool

Why this was built in an authoring tool

My first two case studies were hand-coded. This one was deliberately built in isEazy Author — an industry authoring platform — to demonstrate practical fluency with the kind of tools instructional designers use day to day. I adapted the platform's slide templates and layouts to fit the content's structure, generated supporting visuals with its built-in AI image tool, and built the knowledge checks with its quiz interactions — including extending the default three-option format to four where the design called for an additional distractor. The wider point for a hiring team: learning one authoring tool independently means onboarding onto another — Articulate, Captivate — is a short step, not a training cost.
